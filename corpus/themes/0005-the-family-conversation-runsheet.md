# The family conversation
## Run sheet · 45 minutes

**The ask:** we have extraordinary range across this family, in far more than tech. How do we bring it together?

**The measure:** people say something out loud about what they would want to do.

**The quiet test:** whether people leave feeling heard and part of something, rather than recruited.

Everything before the ask exists to make it easy to answer. Not easy to accept. **Easy to answer**, because a question about what we could build together does not need anybody to commit to anything.

---

## The one rule

**Talk less than everyone else combined.** Roughly 15 minutes of you out of 45. If you reach the end having spoken for 30, it went wrong however well you spoke.

Write the number on a bit of paper in front of you.

If the room opens early at minute 18 and runs, let it. Cut the visioning to two minutes. That trade is always worth taking.

## 3–6 · Thanks, and the honest bit

> "Before anything else, thank you for making the time. I know how much is going on for all of you, in every direction, and carving out three quarters of an hour on a normal week is not nothing.
>
> One thing before I start. I've built a lot of this out. Partly that's had to happen, and honestly, partly it's just me. When I'm excited about something I run at it. That's how I am, and I don't think that's a bad thing.
>
> But I know I don't know everything, and I'm not right about everything, even when I've got strong views. And when someone shows you a lot of finished-looking stuff, I know it can land as 'here's the thing' rather than 'what do you reckon'. That's genuinely not where I'm at with it.
>
> What actually matters to me is what we could build together. So I'd rather show you less today and hear more.
>
> Quick recap of Code Quest, a few things I noticed that I didn't expect, a bit about where this could go, and then something I want to ask all of you. Jump in whenever."

**That last line is a promise. Keep it.**

---

## 6–12 · The recap

Short, warm, evidence first. Do not sell it.

**Where it came from.** It did not start as a coding thing. It started with a family quiz night in March that broke. Arthur reported a bug because he had answered everything right and finished bottom. Lina took over as quiz master and the whole thing froze. And out of that, the offer was: does anyone want to learn how to build one. Ben was the one who said the words *family hackathon*. Mum picked the date, the Saturday of Easter. Elsie and Hannah had already written the questions.

**Code Quest 1, 4 April.** Six kids, three cities, two weeks of them working on their own. Four apps, all live, all working. Seven badges. Emma got an honorary one at four.

**Code Quest 2, 1 August.** One room, one day. Six builds that did not exist that morning. Every child stood up and explained theirs. Mum judged it publicly and it ended in a tie.

Then the two proofs, quickly:

1. Open one of the games on your phone. Ten seconds of it being played.
2. Open `math-kingdom.app.builtby.dev.olatoye.net`. Say plainly: that is on the real internet, at its own address, anyone in the world can open it, and Ben built the entire thing that puts it there.

**Then close the laptop.**

## 12–18 · What I noticed

The part only you can give them.

- **Mum gave out seven awards and not one of them was about code.** Best presenter, best team leader, most creative, for overcoming, calm under pressure, perseverance, participation. Nobody told her what to look for. That is just what she saw.
- **In April I asked each of them what they had learned.** Elsie did not say a database or GitHub. She said you need confidence and to present clearly. She was nine.
- **The best moment was not the building, it was the standing up.** Kids who did not want to speak, spoke.
- **It did not stop when the day stopped.** Four of the six have carried on building since August with nothing helping them. Elsie made another game and then made a video about her own studio that nobody suggested. Emma, at four, went home and built a game having been too young to even compete.
- **The parents in the room got more out of it than we expected.** Watching your own child explain something they made is different from being told they did well.

Then hand over, **here, not at the end**:

> "That's what I saw, but I was in the middle of it. What did you see that I would have missed?"

**Then stop.** If nobody speaks, go round rather than picking someone: "I want to hear from everyone on this, so I'll just go round." A fixed order means nobody is singled out, everybody knows their turn is coming, and nobody has to fight for a gap on a video call.

Do not fill the silence. Count to ten first.

---

## 18–22 · Where this could go

Two altitudes, briefly. Hold both loosely.

**Near.** What Ben built is not a demo. A child uploads, a parent approves, it goes live at its own address. Which means this does not have to be one day a year. And delivery does not have to mean a Saturday either: recorded content, explainers, something someone films once and it lives on there.

**Longer.** As a possibility, not a plan.

> "There's no reason this only works for our kids. If a day like that works with twenty children who are not related to us, then it's something real. Whether that becomes a small thing we do a few times a year or something bigger, I honestly don't know, and I don't think we need to know today."

Naming your own uncertainty does more work here than confidence would.

---

## 22–25 · The ask

Slow down. This is what the meeting exists for.

> "So here's what I actually want to ask, and it's bigger than Code Quest.
>
> The lines have fallen for us in pleasant places. There's serious range across this family, and hardly any of it is tech. Between the four houses there's experience in things I couldn't begin to list, and I'm not going to try, because I'd miss people out and I'd get it wrong.
>
> But one chases a thousand and two put ten thousand to flight. And we've got three generations here. Mum, us, and the kids.
>
> So the question I want to put to everyone is just: **how do we bring all of that together?**
>
> The nearest practical thing is the next Code Quest. But honestly that's just the closest thing to hand, not necessarily the best one. If there's something better we could point all this at, I'd rather hear that."

**Then be quiet.** Properly quiet. However long it takes.

---

## 25–40 · Theirs

**No jobs list.** A list of slots is recruitment wearing a question's clothes, and people can tell. Three moves instead.

### You go first, on your gaps

Not what you bring, everyone knows what you bring.

> "I'll go first. What I'm actually good at is building things fast and getting them out. What I'm not good at is slowing down. And I'm not the one who makes a nervous kid feel alright in a room. There's loads I haven't got."

Naming what you lack is what makes room for someone else.

### Ask what they'd enjoy, not what they're good at

> "If you could put your hand to any part of something like this, what would you actually enjoy doing? Not what you'd be useful for. What you'd enjoy."

Good-at forces either a boast or a modesty performance, and in a family both are awkward. Desire is easier to answer honestly, and it self-selects for what lasts. People sustain what they like and quietly drop what they were merely useful for.

### Ask what's missing

> "And what do you think we haven't got? Because there'll be things we'd need that none of us can do."

Pointing at a gap is easier than volunteering yourself, and it often ends with someone saying "well, actually, I could probably do that."

### While they talk

1. **Write down what people say where they can see it.** Then say it back. Being written down is what makes someone feel it counted.
2. **Never upgrade an offer.** If someone says they could look at the photos, that is brilliant and it goes on the list. Do not say "you could own all the content." Upgrading is how you scare people off.
3. **Welcome disagreement loudly.** Anyone who criticises it is already treating it as theirs.

### If it stays quiet

Do not push. Name it.

> "I might be putting this badly. What would make this worth your time, if anything?"

---

## 40–45 · Close small

> "I don't want anyone signing up to anything today. Could we each just take one thing to look at before we speak again? Mine is finding out what we'd actually need in place to have other people's children in a room, because that's the thing that blocks everything else."

Go round. One thing each, said out loud, by them.

**Then put the next call in the diary before anyone leaves.** Two to three weeks. A meeting that ends without a date is a meeting that ends.

---

## Not today

**Equity, ownership, percentages, structure.** There is a good answer ready and this is not the day for it. Money in an exploratory family conversation makes people cautious, and cautious people do not volunteer.

**Anything beyond the two proofs.** Not the deck, the positioning work, the ladder, the 3D world, the app mockups. All good, all of it says "this is finished and I've thought of everything", which is the opposite of an invitation. If someone asks, offer to send it after.

**Defending anything.** If someone raises something you solved six weeks ago, "that's a fair point" costs nothing. Winning a point tomorrow loses the meeting.

**Promises in front of the children.** Nothing about future events said where kids will remember it.

---

## What a good ending looks like

Three people said something you had not thought of. Two named something they would want to do. Somebody disagreed with you out loud. There is a date in the diary.

And nobody left feeling recruited.
