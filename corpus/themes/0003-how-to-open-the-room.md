# How to open the room

**Status:** settled
**Date:** 16 August 2026
**Context:** written for the family conversation of 17 August, but it holds for any moment where a lot of finished work meets people who were not holding the pen.

## The problem

The volume of work is itself the problem. Everything built so far is good, and the more polished it looks, the louder it says *decided*. Even where people were consulted, output at scale changes what consultation feels like.

So showing less is not a presentation tactic. It is the whole thing.

## The move

Do not explain yourself and do not apologise. Make it collective from the first sentence, and move the subject immediately from what has been built to what could be built together.

The temptation is to address the imbalance directly, to name that a lot has been built by one person. Resist it. Naming it makes it the subject. Speaking collectively makes it a non-issue.

## The words

> "Before I start, we've got this to a place I don't think any of us expected. Two events, six kids building real things, and something actually live on the internet.
>
> What I want to talk about today is less about what's been done, and more about what we could do together. Because I think there's more here than any one of us can see on our own.
>
> So I'd rather show you less today and hear more."

## Why it works

It is collective from the first word. "We've got this to a place" credits everyone without listing anyone, which avoids both the flattery problem and the risk of missing someone out.

It also removes the self-justification entirely. An earlier draft explained the pace, that when excited about something you run at it, which was true but made the opening about one person and gave the room something to react to. The version above gives them nothing to absolve and nothing to resent. It simply moves the subject from what has been done to what could be done.

## The commitment it creates

Having said "I'd rather hear more", the recap must genuinely be short and the silence after the observations must be real. Saying it and then talking for twenty five minutes does more damage than never saying it.

## The matching close

If the opening softens, the ask softens with it. Not a proposal with a shape already decided, but an invitation with the shape still open:

> "So what I'm really asking is, would there be an appetite for us to do the next one together? And if there would be, what would that even look like? Because I genuinely don't know, and I'd rather work it out with you than turn up with it."

The last clause is the whole meeting in one line. It offers people the thing that matters most, which is shaping it rather than joining it.

## The general principle

Ask first, and ask by name, whoever is most likely to feel they were not consulted. Being asked first signals status in a way nothing else does.
